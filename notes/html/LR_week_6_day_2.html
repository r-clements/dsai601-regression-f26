I. Leverage (recap)

Carried over from Week 6, Day 1: hii is the ith diagonal entry of the n×n hat matrix H.

  1. 0≤hii≤1
  2. ∑ihii=tr(H)=p,h‾=pn

Rule of thumb High leverage: hii>3pn. With p=10 and n=100 that is 30100 — flag any point whose leverage exceeds 0.3.

Long description of Figure 33

The dotted line is the least-squares fit to the cluster alone, with a slope of about 2. The solid line is the fit to all nine points, and its slope collapses to about 0.3 — dragged down at the right by that single observation. II. Outliers

Externally studentized residual

stud(ei)= ei MSE(i) (1−hii) ∼ t(n−1)−p
What each piece of that ratio is, as annotated in the original
PieceWhat it is
ei the raw residual, yi−y^i
1−hii the leverage correction
MSE(i) from the model fit on all observations except the ith point
MSE(i)= σ^(i)2= SSE(i) (n−1)−p = ∑jej2 (n−1)−p

The degrees of freedom are (n−1)−p, not n−p, because the model is fit on n−1 points.

Where the denominator comes from:

Var(e) = σ2(I−H) SE^(e) = σ^2(I−H) ⟹SE^(ei) = σ^(i)2(1−hii)

The subscript (i) is what makes it externally studentized — the ith point is left out of the variance estimate, so a genuine outlier does not get to inflate the yardstick used to judge it.

Decision |stud(ei)|>tn−1−p,(1−α/2)* ⟹ the ith point is an outlier. In practice the margin note just says the cutoff is about 2.

III. Influential points

High leverage + outlier. Neither on its own is enough; a point has to be both unusual in x and badly fitted before it moves the answer.

Cook's distance

Di= (yi−y^i)2 pσ^2 · hii (1−hii)2
The original underlines the two factors in different colours; they are the two halves of "influential"
FactorRoleBehaviour
(yi−y^i)2pσ^2 outlier part grows with how badly the point is fitted
hii(1−hii)2 leverage part as hii rises this rises too, and faster — the denominator is shrinking while the numerator grows

Rule of thumb Di>4n ⟹ “influential”.

What to do?

  • Do not delete unless it's erroneous.
  • If not sure, analyze the data twice, with and without. Report both sets of results.
  • Try a different model — new data, a different method.

V. The problem with R²

As you add predictors, R2 goes up. The geometry says why it has no choice.

Long description of Figure 34

A parallelogram drawn in perspective represents Col(X1,X2), the two-dimensional subspace spanned by both predictors. A thick line drawn inside it is Col(X1), the one-dimensional subspace spanned by the first predictor alone — note that the line lies within the plane.

From a dot marking the origin, a vector rises out of the plane to the point Y at the top right. Two residual vectors run back down from Y: a dashed one to y^1, the point on the line, and a solid one to y^2, the point in the plane, which meets the plane at a right angle marked with a small square. A faint dotted segment inside the plane joins y^1 to y^2.

Those three segments form a right triangle, which is the whole argument: the solid residual is a leg and the dashed one is the hypotenuse, so the solid one cannot be longer. Dashed versus solid carries the same distinction as the two pen colours in the original.

The two statements written beside the figure:

‖e2‖ ≤ ‖e1‖
‖e1‖2 = ‖e2‖2 + ‖y^2−y^1‖2

The second is Pythagoras on that right triangle, and it forces the first: ‖e1‖2 is ‖e2‖2 plus something that cannot be negative. Since SSE=‖e‖2, adding a predictor can never raise SSE, so it can never lower R2.

Demo to come We'll show that 19 junk predictors give R2≈.79.

Adjusted R2 charges for the extra parameter, so that particular disaster can't happen:

adj R2=1− SSE/(n−p) SST/(n−1)

But it is not a cure. Adjusted R2 still increases for insignificant predictors — anything with p-val<.32. It overfits, and adds too many predictors.

VI. Mallows' Cp

Two issues with models:

  1. Bias in predictions
  2. Variance in predictions

The expected (standardized) prediction error charges for both:

Γp= 1σ2 ∑i E(y^i−E(yi))2

with E(yi) the true mean response. Splitting that into variance and squared bias:

Γp= 1σ2 [ ∑iVar(y^i) + ∑i(E(y^i)−E(yi))2 ]
The two bracketed terms, labelled (a) and (b) in the original
TermNameEvaluates to
(a) variance Var(Y^)=σ2H⟹∑iVar(y^i)=σ2tr(H)=σ2p
(b) bias squared E(SSEp)−(n−p)σ2

Substituting both into the bracket, the σ2s cancel against the leading 1σ2 — the original strikes them out in red — and replacing the unknowns by their estimates leaves:

Cp= SSEp σ^F2 −(n−2p)

The full model always lands on the diagonal. Putting SSEF and σ^F2=SSEF/(n−pmax) into the formula gives

Cpmax= SSEF SSEF/(n−pmax) −(n−2pmax) =pmax

Reading the Cp plot

Long description of Figure 35

The horizontal axis is p, the number of parameters, ticked 1 to 5; the vertical axis is Cp, with 5 marked. A straight reference line rises from the bottom left: this is Cp=p, and the full model sits exactly on it at p=5, as the algebra above guarantees.

Each candidate model is a point. The intercept-only model sits at the top left, far above the line, annotated high bias; X1 and X2 at p=2 are lower but still well above it, and X1,X2 at p=3 is above it too. Two points fall below the line: X1,X3 at p=3, which is ringed, and X1,X2,X3 at p=4.

Distance above the line is bias, so the rule is to take the smallest p whose Cp is below or on the line. Both of the low points qualify on bias, and the ringed one wins because it is further left — the same reading the original marks with a ring and the word parsimonious.

Rule. Smallest p such that Cp is below or on the line ⟹ low bias. Parsimonious.

VII. AIC — Akaike information criterion

Assume ε∼N(0,σ2I). The log-likelihood is

ℓ(β,σ2)= −n2ln(2π) −n2ln(σ2) −12σ2 ‖Y−Xβ‖22

Evaluated at the maximum likelihood estimates, where σ^2=SSEp/n, this collapses to

−2ℓ(β^,σ^2)= nlnSSEp −nlnn +nln2π +n
AIC= −2ℓ(β^,σ^2) +2(p+1)

Only the first two terms depend on the model, so comparing AIC across candidates is comparing nlnSSEp+2(p+1) — fit against a penalty of 2 per parameter. The +1 counts σ2, which is estimated too.
