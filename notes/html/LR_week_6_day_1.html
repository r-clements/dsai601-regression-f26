I. Multicollinearity

Two or more predictors are highly / perfectly correlated.

Write the design matrix out with its columns named:

The n×p design matrix X, an intercept column followed by the p−1 predictor columns
Row 1 X1 X2 ⋯ Xp−1
11 X11 X21 ⋯ X(p−1)1
21 X12 X22 ⋮
⋮⋮⋮⋮⋱
n1 X1n X2n ⋯ X(p−1)n

The original braces the predictor columns and asks: what if one of these is linearly dependent (a linear combination), or close to it, on the others?

Exactly dependent columns look like this:

X1 = X2+X3 X2 = 5X6+7X7
β^= (XTX)−1 XTY

The notes underline XTX and warn that it might have issues — that factor is where the whole problem lands.

Same model, different βs

Take y∼X1+X2, so that Y=Xβ+ε with β=(β0,β1,β2) — but X2=4X1.

Suppose we know the truth is β=(1,2,4). Substituting the dependency:

yi= 1+2X1i+4X2i+εi yi= 1+2X1i+4(4X1i)+εi yi= 1+18X1i+0X2i+εi

A third choice does the same thing:

yi= 1+22X1i−X2i+εi = 1+22X1i−4X1i+εi = 1+18X1i+εi
Three different β vectors, one identical fitted model
β Written out After substituting X2=4X1
(1, 2, 4) — the true model 1+2X1+4X2 1+18X1
(1, 18, 0) 1+18X1+0X2 1+18X1
(1, 22, −1) 1+22X1−X2 1+18X1

Key point All three produce exactly the same fitted values, so the data cannot tell them apart. Which one is right? There is no answer — and that is the point.

Three consequences

① Non-identifiability

β^=(XTX)−1XTY could be impossible to calculate, or very unstable, because of division by ≈0.

② Inflated variance

Var(β^)= σ2 (XTX)−1

This explodes — the variances of the β^ are inflated. The margin note gives the reason:

Why An inverse is 1determinant times a matrix. Near-dependent columns drive the determinant toward zero, and dividing by something near zero blows the whole thing up.

③ Inference

t= β^i−0 SE^(β^i)

The denominator is squiggle-underlined in the original with the note “this is huge”. A huge standard error makes t always really small — never reject H0. Low power.

Symptoms

Adding a predictor increases the SE of the others, or changes the βs by “a lot”.

The same two models fitted under low and high correlation between X1 and X2
Model corr≈0: β^1 corr≈0: β^2 corr≈1: β^1 corr≈1: β^2
y∼X1 −1—−1—
y∼X1+X2 −1.1−510−4

Under low correlation, adding X2 nudges β^1 from −1 to −1.1. Under high correlation the same move sends it from −1 to +10 — a sign flip and an order of magnitude. That is the symptom.

② Variance inflation factor (VIF)

VIF measures the inflation of the variance of β^j when you add Xj to the model.

VIF= 1 1−Rj2

where Rj2 is the R2 from regressing Xj on every other predictor:

Xj∼ X1+ X2+⋯+ Xj−1+ Xj+1+⋯+ Xp−1
  • If Rj2 is high, Xj is close to a linear combination of the other predictors, and VIF goes up.
  • If VIF=1, there is no correlation with the other predictors.
Rules of thumb
RangeSeverity
1≤VIF≤4 light
4<VIF≤10 moderate
VIF>10 severe

Solutions

  1. Drop a correlated predictor, keep one.
  2. Feature engineering — build X*=g(correlated Xs).
  3. Regularized regression — LASSO, Ridge.
  4. Partial least squares.
  5. PCA.

The original brackets items 3 through 5 in the margin as “Intro to ML / advanced ML” — flagged as material for a later course rather than this one.

II. Outliers and influential points

Leverage

Y^= Hn×n Yn×1

Taking the ith row of H against the whole of Y:

y^i= hi1y1+ hi2y2+ ⋯+ hinyn

So hii = the influence of yi on y^i.

hii is the leverage of the ith point, and is a measure of the distance between the Xs at the ith point and the average Xs.

In SLR that distance is explicit:

hii= 1n+ (Xi−x‾)2 SSX

Properties of hii

  1. 0≤hii≤1
  2. ∑ihii=p,h‾=pn

Rule of thumb High leverage: hii>3pn — three times the average leverage.

Long description of Figure 32

Axes of y against x. Eight observations cluster in the left half of the plot and trend upward steeply. One further observation sits far out to the right, circled, and well below the level the cluster's trend would predict for it.

Two fitted lines are drawn, both least-squares fits to the plotted data. The dotted line, labelled "without it", is the fit to the cluster alone — it is steep, with a slope of about 2, and passes through the middle of the cluster. The solid line, labelled "with it", is the fit to all nine points, and its slope collapses to about 0.3: it is dragged down at the right by the single distant observation and lifted at the left as a consequence, so it now misses the cluster entirely.

The isolated point has high leverage because its x value is far from x‾, which is exactly what the (Xi−x‾)2SSX term measures. Dotted versus solid carries the same distinction as the two line styles in the original.
