I. The partial F-test

Compare a full model against a reduced model nested inside it — the reduced model is the full model with some subset of predictors deleted.

F= (SSER−SSEF) /(p−k) SSEF/(n−p) ∼ Fp−k,n−p
What the two counts mean
SymbolCounts
p number of βs in the full model
k number of βs in the reduced model

So p−k is the number of coefficients the reduced model leaves out, and that is the numerator degrees of freedom.

Long description of Figure 26

A right-skewed density drawn over a horizontal axis with a vertical axis at the left. The curve starts at the origin, climbs steeply to a peak in the left third, then decays slowly to the right, flattening as it approaches the axis. The large area to the left is labelled 1−α. A short vertical line near the right-hand end marks the critical value Fp−k,n−p*, and the small hatched sliver beyond it has area α. Dropping predictors can only make SSE larger, so the numerator is never negative and only large values of F count as evidence — the rejection region is entirely one-sided even though each individual null is two-sided.

Decision. If F>F*, reject H0: at least one predictor left out of the reduced model is a significant predictor of Y, conditional on the other predictors being in the model.

Specific predictor tests

The special case where the reduced model drops exactly one predictor.

H0 : βi=0 H1 : βi≠0
F= (SSER−SSEF) /1 SSEF/(n−p) ∼ F1,n−p

Only one coefficient is deleted, so p−k=1 and the numerator degrees of freedom collapse to 1.

Relation to the t-statistic for a coefficient

t= β^i−0 SE^(β^i)

where

SE^(β^i) = Var(β^i) = σ^2 [(XTX)−1] i,i

The subscript i,i picks the ith diagonal entry. Scaled by σ^2, the whole diagonal of that matrix is the list of coefficient variances:

( Var(β^0) ⋱ Var(β^p−1) )

Note: t2=F. The two-sided t test for a single coefficient and the partial F test that drops that one predictor are the same test.

Decision. If |t|>tn−1*, reject H0: Xi is a significant predictor of y, conditional on the other predictors being in the model.

II. Adjusted R²

Recall:

R2=1− SSESST
Long description of Figure 27

Six observations are plotted against a predictor. A solid rising line is the fitted regression line y^; a dashed horizontal line is the sample mean y‾. Three of the points sit above the fitted line and three below it, and three sit above the mean line and three below, so neither set of deviations is systematically one-signed.

From every point two vertical segments are drawn. The solid segment runs to the fitted line: squaring and adding those gives SSE, the variation the model has failed to explain. The dashed segment runs to the mean line: squaring and adding those gives SST, the total variation in y. Toward the right of the plot the dashed segments are long while the solid ones stay short — that gap is what the regression has explained, and R2 reports it as a fraction. Solid versus dashed carries the same distinction as the two pen colours in the original notes, so the figure does not depend on colour.

R2 increases with additional predictors. That makes it useless for choosing between models of different sizes — adding junk never makes it go down. The fix is to charge each extra parameter a price by dividing each sum of squares by its degrees of freedom:

Radj2=1− SSE/(n−p) SST/(n−1)

Asked in class What if p=1 — the intercept-only model?

Why the penalty bites:

  1. As p increases, n−p shrinks, so SSE/(n−p) tends to increase.
  2. The denominator SST/(n−1) does not depend on p at all, so the whole ratio increases.
  3. Therefore Radj2 decreases as predictors are added — unless the new predictor cuts SSE by enough to outweigh the lost degree of freedom.

III. Feature engineering

Transformations

yi= β0+ β1Xi+ εi

The model is linear in the parameters, but our Xs can be nonlinear. That is the licence for everything below.

① Logs. Three arrangements, with three different readings of β1:

yi= β0+ β1log(Xi)+ εi
log(yi)= β0+ β1Xi+ εi

Interpretation For the log-y model just above: β1 ≈ % change in y per 1-unit change in X.

log(yi)= β0+ β1log(Xi)+ εi

Caution Be careful if X or y≤0.

Long description of Figure 28

A pair of axes with the curve of log(x) drawn only for positive x. Reading from the right, the curve is almost flat and rises very slowly; moving left it drops through the horizontal axis at x=1, marked with a dot, and then falls increasingly steeply, running off the bottom of the plot as x nears zero. The region at and to the left of the vertical axis is empty and annotated "undefined for x≤0". So a single zero or negative value in a column is enough to break a log transformation of that column, and values just above zero become extreme outliers after the transform.

② Polynomials

yi= β0+ β1Xi+ β2Xi2+ εi

Not the same interpretation. Once X2 is in the model, β1 is no longer "the change in y per unit change in X" — you cannot move X while holding X2 fixed.

Terms of different kinds mix freely in one model:

y= β0+ β1X1+ β2X12+ β3X2+ β4log(X3)+ ⋯

Caution X and X2 are correlated. Center X first by taking X−X‾.

IV. Categorical predictors

Insurance data, regressing price on region:

price= β0+ β1region

with

region={ Northeast, Northwest, Southeast, Southwest }

A K-level categorical predictor becomes K−1 dummy variables. Here K=4, so region contributes three columns, not four.

This is one-hot encoding. Each row of X carries a 1 in the column for its own region — except the level that gets no column of its own, which is recorded as zeros everywhere:

The design matrix X for K=4 regions. The final column is not part of X; it names the level each row encodes, which the original notes marked with highlighter.
Intercept NW SE SW Region this row encodes
1000Northeast — reference level
1100Northwest
1010Southeast
1001Southwest
⋮⋮⋮⋮⋮

Northeast has no column. A row of all zeros in NW, SE and SW is the Northeast encoding, so Northeast is absorbed into the intercept and every other region's coefficient reads as a difference from Northeast. Giving all four levels a column would make them sum to the intercept column, and XTX would not be invertible — which is why it is K−1 columns and not K.
