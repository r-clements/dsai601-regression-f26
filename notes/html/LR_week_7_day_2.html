II. Parameter estimation

We need maximum likelihood estimation. Start from the Bernoulli probability mass function, written as a single expression:

The two cases the single formula has to cover
ValueProbability
1πi
01−πi
P(Yi=yi)= πiyi (1−πi)1−yi

The exponents act as switches. Put yi=1 and the second factor becomes (·)0=1, leaving πi; put yi=0 and the first factor switches off instead, leaving 1−πi. One formula, both rows of the table.

Observations are independent, so the likelihood is the product:

L(β)= ∏i=1n πiyi (1−πi)1−yi

and taking logs turns the product into a sum:

ℓ(β) = ∑i=1n ln( πiyi (1−πi)1−yi ) = ∑i=1n [ yilnπi + (1−yi) ln(1−πi) ]

Maximising ℓ(β) is the same as minimising −ℓ(β), and the rest of the session works with the negative version because optimisers are written to go downhill.

No closed form

Differentiating the negative log-likelihood gives

∂[−ℓ(β)]∂β = −XT(Y−π)

where Y is the n×1 vector of observed 0s and 1s and π is the n×1 vector of fitted probabilities π1,…,πn.

The problem There is no closed-form solution for β. Setting that derivative to zero gives equations in which β is buried inside π, and they cannot be unpicked. We must use a numerical optimization method.

Two named methods appear in the notes:

  • Newton–Raphson method
  • Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS)

Contrast this with ordinary least squares, where setting the derivative to zero gave the normal equations and β^=(XTX)−1XTY straight away. That convenience is gone here.

III. Gradient descent

Long description of Figure 38

A convex, bowl-shaped curve showing −ℓ(β1) against β1. Five dots sit on the curve, joined by a dashed path and labelled step 1 through step 4. The first dot is high on the left wall; each subsequent dot is further right and lower down, and the gaps between them shrink markedly — the first step covers most of the distance and the fourth is barely visible. The path converges on the lowest point of the bowl, marked as the minimum, which sits at β^1 on the horizontal axis.

The shrinking is not a choice, it is automatic: each step is proportional to the slope, and the slope goes to zero at the bottom. These are the actual iterates of β(t+1)=β(t)−αf′ on a quadratic, not a freehand sketch.

The derivative carries two pieces of information, and gradient descent uses both.

The learning rate

Second piece: the derivative also has a magnitude, and we use it to help adjust β. For better control of the step size, introduce α, the learning rate:

β1(t+1) = β1(t) − α ddβ1 [−ℓ(β1)]

Two panels, each showing the same bowl-shaped −ℓ(β1) curve.

Both panels show genuine gradient descent runs on a quadratic — the left uses a learning rate above the stability threshold, the right one far below the useful range.

Gradient descent in two dimensions

Direction. With more than one parameter we have more options, and we want the steepest descent. The individual slopes are the partial derivatives ∂∂βi[−ℓ(β)], and combining them into a vector gives the gradient ∇[−ℓ(β)]:

∇[−ℓ(β)] = ( ∂∂β1 [−ℓ(β(t))] ∂∂β2 [−ℓ(β(t))] )
Long description of Figure 40

A pair of axes, β1 across and β2 up, each ticked at 1, 2 and 3. A solid arrow runs from the origin up and to the right, ending at the point 1 across and 2 up, and is labelled "gradient = (1, 2)". Two dotted segments trace its components: one along the horizontal axis out to 1, then one vertically up to 2.

The point of the picture is that the two partial derivatives are not two separate answers — stacked into a vector they define one direction. Gradient descent moves against that arrow, which is the direction of steepest descent.

Vanilla gradient descent

β(t+1) = β(t) − α ∇[−ℓ(β(t))]

And that gradient is the quantity from earlier in the session:

∇[−ℓ(β)] = −XT(Y−π)

so every step is computable even though the equation XT(Y−π)=0 cannot be solved in closed form. Notice the shape of Y−π: observed minus fitted, the same residual pattern that drives the normal equations in ordinary least squares.
