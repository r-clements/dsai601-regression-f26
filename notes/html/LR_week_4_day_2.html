Reading the covariance matrix of β^. It is p×p, with the individual variances down the diagonal:

Var(β^)= ( Var(β^0) ⋱ Var(β^p−1) )

The off-diagonal entries are the covariances between pairs of estimates — they are not zero in general, which is exactly why a coefficient's meaning depends on what else is in the model.

I. Distribution of the fitted values

Y^= Xβ^= X(XTX)−1XTY =HY

Since Y^ is a fixed matrix times a normal vector it is normal too, so all we need are its mean and its variance.

The mean

E(Y^)= E(Xβ^)= XE(β^)= Xβ

The variance

Rule For a constant matrix A and random vector W,

Var(AW)=AVar(W)AT
Var(Y^) = Var(HY) = HVar(Y)HT = Hσ2IHT = σ2HHT = σ2H

The last two steps use two facts about H that we prove next: it is symmetric, so HT=H, and it is idempotent, so HH=H. Putting the two pieces together:

Y^∼N (Xβ,σ2H)

Transcription note The handwritten notes write Y^∼N(,) with the two slots left blank, to be filled in from the mean and variance derived underneath. The completed statement above is what those derivations give.

H is a projection matrix

A matrix A is a projection matrix if two conditions hold:

  1. A is symmetric, AT=A;
  2. A is idempotent, A2=A.

1. Symmetry

(X(XTX)−1XT)T = X[(XTX)−1]TXT = X[(XTX)T]−1XT = X(XTX)−1XT = H

The step that makes it work (XTX)T=XTX. Transpose and inverse also commute, (A−1)T=(AT)−1, which is what lets the transpose move inside the inverse in the second line.

2. Idempotence

H2= X(XTX)−1 XTX⏟ cancels the inverse (XTX)−1XT = X(XTX)−1XT=H

So H is a projection matrix. Idempotence is the algebraic form of an obvious geometric fact: once you have projected Y onto the column space, projecting again changes nothing, because you are already there.

Residuals are orthogonal to fitted values

Long description of Figure 24

The same projection picture as the previous lecture, now labelled with the two matrices. A slanted parallelogram is the column space of X, spanned by two in-plane axes x1 and x2, and p-dimensional. A long vector Y rises out of the plane from the origin; the observed responses live in n dimensions and do not generally lie in the plane. Inside the plane sits Y^=Xβ^=HY. A dashed segment runs from its tip up to the tip of Y, labelled e=Y−Y^=(I−H)Y, and a small square marks the right angle where it meets the plane.

The two matrices split the space between them: H sends Y to the part living in the column space, and I−H sends it to the part at right angles to that space, of dimension n−p. The right angle in the picture is precisely the claim eTY^=0, which the derivation below confirms.

The orthogonality we drew is an algebraic identity. Writing the sum as an inner product:

∑ieiy^i =0= ⟨e,Y^⟩ = eTY^ = [(I−H)Y]THY = YT(I−H)THY = YT(I−H)HY = YT(H−H2)Y = YT(H−H)Y=0

Both projection properties are used: symmetry turns (I−H)T into I−H, and idempotence collapses H−H2 to zero.

e=Y−Y^ =Y−HY =(I−H)Y

The variance

e∼N (0,σ2(I−H))

I−H is a projection matrix too. The collapse in the last line is idempotence again — if H2=H then (I−H)2=I−2H+H2=I−H. Note also that the covariance matrix is not diagonal: the residuals are correlated with one another even though the errors are not.

The divisor is n−p, not n−2 — one degree of freedom is spent per coefficient estimated. It is the dimension of the orthogonal complement in Figure 24.

III. The global F-test

The question: are any of my predictors X1,…,Xp−1 significant predictors of Y?

H0: β1=β2=⋯=βp−1=0 H1: at least one βj≠0

Written as a comparison of two models rather than a statement about parameters:

The global F-test as reduced versus full
HypothesisModelName
H0 yi=β0+εi Null / reduced model R
H1 yi=β0+β1x1i+⋯+βp−1x(p−1)i+εi Full model F

Why intercept only Setting every slope to zero leaves yi=β0+εi, whose least squares fit is y‾. So the global F-test asks whether the model beats predicting with the mean.

The statistic

In SLR this was

F= SSR/1 SSE/(n−2) = (SST−SSE)/1 SSE/(n−2)

and in MLR it becomes

F= (SSER−SSEF)/(p−1) SSEF/(n−p) ∼ Fp−1,n−p

with p the number of β's, and SST=SSE+SSR as before.

Long description of Figure 25

A right-skewed density over a horizontal axis: it starts at the origin, climbs to a peak in the left third, and decays slowly to the right. The bulk of the area, to the left, is labelled 1−α. A short vertical line near the right-hand end marks the critical value, labelled Fp−1,n−p*, and the hatched sliver beyond it has area α. The statistic is a ratio of two non-negative mean squares, so it can never be negative, and only large values are evidence against the null — hence a single upper tail even though the null hypothesis is two-sided in each βj.

Decision. If F>Fp−1,n−p*(1−α), reject H0: at least one predictor is a significant predictor of Y.

Note what this does not tell you — which one.

The same statistic, for any reduced versus full pair

F= (SSER−SSEF)/(p−k) SSEF/(n−p)

where k is the number of β's in the reduced model. The global test is the special case k=1 — the reduced model keeps only the intercept.

The ANOVA table

ANOVA — analysis of variance — is just this comparison laid out as a table.

ANOVA table for a reduced-versus-full comparison

Where F comes from in the table The F column is the Regression mean square divided by the Error mean square — the top row over the middle row. In the original notes this is drawn as two arrows, one from each mean square, meeting at the F column.

IV. The partial F-test

Instead of testing every slope at once, test a subset — say the last few, Xr,Xr+1,…,Xp−1.

H0: βr=βr+1=⋯=βp−1=0 H1: at least one is not 0
The partial F-test as reduced versus full
HypothesisModel
H0 (reduced) yi=β0+β1x1i+⋯+βr−1x(r−1)i+εi
H1 (full) yi=β0+β1x1i+⋯+βp−1x(p−1)i+εi
F= (SSER−SSEF)/(p−r)⏟difference in # of βs SSEF/(n−p) ∼ Fp−r,n−p

Transcription note The notes use k for "number of β's in the reduced model" in the generic formula and the ANOVA table, then r here for the index of the first dropped predictor. These are the same quantity: dropping Xr onward leaves β0,…,βr−1, which is r coefficients, so k=r and p−k=p−r.

Decision. If F>Fp−r,n−p*(1−α), reject H0: at least one of {Xr,…,Xp−1} is a significant predictor of Y — conditional on X1,…,Xr−1 already being in the model.

That final clause is the whole point of the word partial. A predictor can look significant on its own and not survive here, or the reverse.

The three tests side by side

One statistic, three uses — only the pair of models changes
Test Reduced model Numerator df
Global F intercept only p−1
Partial F (subset) drop Xr,…,Xp−1 p−r
Partial F (one predictor) drop a single Xj 1

The denominator is always SSEF/(n−p), the full model's MSE. With a single dropped predictor the numerator has one degree of freedom, and that F is the square of the t statistic for that coefficient.
