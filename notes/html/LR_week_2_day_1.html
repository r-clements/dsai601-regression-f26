Something we missed: the line passes through the means

Start from the closed-form intercept:

β0 = y‾ − β1 x‾

Rearranging it gives

y‾ = β0 + β1 x‾

The point of means is on the line. Because y‾=β0+β1x‾, the pair (x‾,y‾) satisfies the equation of the regression line — so (x‾,y‾) is on the regression line.

I. Recap

The simple linear regression model:

yi = β0 + β1 xi + εi

The least squares estimates are the pair that minimizes the criterion Q:

( β^0 , β^1 ) = argmin β0,β1 Q (β0,β1)

where

Q(β0,β1) = ∑i=1n (yi−y^i)2 = ∑i=1n (yi−β0−β1xi)2

This quantity is the sum of squared residuals, also written SSE / SSR.

Closed form solutions

β^1 = ∑ixiyi −nx‾y‾ ∑ixi2 −nx‾2
β^0 = y‾ − β^1 x‾

Homework 1

Show that

β^1 = ∑i (xi−x‾) (yi−y‾) ∑i (xi−x‾)2 = Cov(x,y) Var(x)

Just show that this expression is equivalent to the one we derived.

II. Properties of the least squares estimates

Least squares gave us β^0 and β^1. Are these estimates good?

The Gauss–Markov theorem

Under the SLR assumptions, the least squares (LS) estimates β^0 and β^1 are:

1. Unbiased for the parameters

E(β^0) = β0 E(β^1) = β1
Long description of Figure 5

The horizontal axis represents the possible values that the estimator β^0 can take across repeated samples. A symmetric bell-shaped density is drawn above it. A dashed vertical line drops from the peak of the density to a tick on the axis, and that tick is labelled β0. The pedagogical point is that the centre of the sampling distribution coincides with the true parameter: individual estimates are sometimes too high and sometimes too low, but on average they are correct. Nothing in the figure says anything yet about how wide the distribution is — that is the subject of the next property.

2. Best linear unbiased estimators (BLUE)

The LS estimators are the best linear unbiased estimators — "best" meaning smallest variance:

Var(β^0) ≤ Var(β~0)

where β~0 is some other linear unbiased estimate.

Long description of Figure 6

Two densities are drawn on a common horizontal axis, both centred on the same tick, labelled β0. The first is drawn with a solid stroke and is tall and narrow: this is the sampling distribution of the least squares estimator β^0. The second is drawn with a dashed stroke and is short and wide: this is the sampling distribution of some other linear unbiased estimator β~0. Because both are unbiased, the comparison between them is entirely a comparison of spread. The narrowness of the solid curve is the visual content of the claim Var(β^0)≤Var(β~0): among all linear unbiased estimators, least squares wastes the least information.

Let's prove these

We start with the slope. Show that

E(β^1) = E [ ∑i (xi−x‾) (yi−y‾) ∑i (xi−x‾)2 ] = β1

Notation: SSX, SSXY, and a lemma

Firstly, two named quantities:

Sum-of-squares notation used throughout the proof
Expression Name Read as
∑i(xi−x‾)2 SSX "sum of squares for X"
∑i (xi−x‾) (yi−y‾) SSXY "sum of cross-products of X and Y"

Lemma

SSXY = ∑i (xi−x‾) yi

The y‾ can be dropped from the second factor.

Proof of the lemma

∑i (xi−x‾) (yi−y‾) = ∑i ( xiyi −xiy‾ −x‾yi +x‾y‾ ) = ∑i ( (xi−x‾)yi − (xi−x‾)y‾ ) = ∑i (xi−x‾)yi − ∑i (xi−x‾)y‾

The second sum vanishes. Pull y‾ out of it and use x‾=1n∑ixi:

y‾ ∑i (xi−x‾) = y‾ (nx‾−nx‾) = 0

which proves the lemma. = 0 was marked on this term in the original notes.

Proving the slope is unbiased

Back to the proof. Using the lemma, write the slope as a weighted sum of the responses:

β^1 = SSXYSSX = ∑i (xi−x‾) yi SSX = ∑i ( xi−x‾ SSX ) yi

Key move Think of SSX as a constant. The predictors are fixed, so the whole weight

ki = xi−x‾ SSX

is a constant too. That is what lets it pass through E and Var in everything that follows.

So the least squares slope is a linear combination of the responses:

β^1 = ∑i kiyi

Now take expectations:

E(β^1) = E(∑ikiyi) = ∑iE(kiyi) = ∑ikiE(yi) = ∑iki E [β0+β1xi+εi] = ∑iki [ E(β0) +E(β1xi) +E(εi) ] = ∑iki [β0+β1xi+0] = ∑ikiβ0 + ∑iβ1xiki = β0 ∑iki + β1 ∑ixiki

Two facts finish the job.

The weights sum to zero

∑iki = ∑i xi−x‾SSX = 1SSX ∑i (xi−x‾) = 1SSX [nx‾−nx‾] = 0

The weights times the predictors sum to one

∑ikixi = ∑i (xi−x‾SSX) xi = 1SSX ∑i (xi−x‾) xi

Use the lemma! Apply the lemma with y replaced by x:

∑i (xi−x‾) xi = ∑i (xi−x‾) (xi−x‾) = ∑i (xi−x‾)2 = SSX

Therefore

∑ikixi = 1SSX · SSX = 1

Putting it together

β0 ∑iki + β1 ∑ikixi = 0+β1 = β1

The first sum is 0 and the second is 1, so E(β^1)=β1. The slope estimator is unbiased.

Exercise You do β0. Run the same argument to show E(β^0)=β0.

The variance of the slope estimator

Now the second property. We want to show

Var(β^1) ≤ Var(β~1)

for some other linear unbiased β~1. First, the variance of the least squares slope:

Var(β^1) = Var(∑ikiyi) = ∑iVar(kiyi) = ∑iki2Var(yi) = ∑iki2σ2 = σ2∑iki2

Why the variance splits across the sum The step from Var(∑i·) to a sum of variances assumes the covariance is 0: the yi's are uncorrelated, because the εi's are.

The sum of squared weights simplifies:

∑iki2 = ∑i (xi−x‾SSX)2 = ∑i (xi−x‾)2 SSX2 = 1SSX2 ∑i (xi−x‾)2 = 1SSX2SSX = 1SSX

Which gives the result:

Var(β^1) = σ2SSX

Showing the least squares slope is best

Can we show Var(β~1)≥σ2SSX?

Write the competing estimator with perturbed weights:

β~1 = ∑i (ki+di) yi = ∑i k~i yi

where k~i=ki+di and di≠0.

What "linear" buys us β~1 is a linear combination of y — that is the only structure the competitor is allowed to have, and it is enough to pin down its variance.

Var(β~1) = Var (∑i(ki+di)yi) = ∑i Var [(ki+di)yi] = ∑i (ki+di)2 Var(yi) = σ2 ∑i (ki+di)2 = σ2 ∑i (ki2+2kidi+di2) = σ2 ( ∑iki2 + 2∑ikidi + ∑idi2 )

The two extra terms are together ≥0, because:

The two cross terms, and why neither can reduce the variance
TermValue
∑idi2 >0 — a sum of squares, and the di's are not all zero
2∑ikidi =0

Therefore

σ2 ( ∑iki2 + 2∑ikidi + ∑idi2 ) > σ2 ∑iki2

The right-hand side is exactly Var(β^1), so any other linear unbiased estimator has strictly larger variance. Least squares is best.

III. Summarize

What we have established for the least squares slope
QuantityResult
E(β^1) β1
Var(β^1) σ2SSX

What about the intercept?

Can we show E(β^0)=β0? And Var(β^0)≤Var(β~0)? The variance turns out to be

Var(β^0) = σ2 ( 1n + x‾2SSX )

IV. Sampling distributions of the estimators

If we assume

εi ∼ N (0,σ2)

then the estimators are normally distributed as well:

β^1 ∼ N ( β1 , σ2SSX )
β^0 ∼ N ( β0 , σ2 ( 1n + x‾2SSX ) )

Why this matters. Unbiasedness and the variance formulas hold without any normality assumption — that is the content of Gauss–Markov. Adding εi∼N(0,σ2) upgrades them to full distributions, which is what confidence intervals and hypothesis tests are built on.
