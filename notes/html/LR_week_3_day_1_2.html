II. Confidence interval for the mean response

An interval such that the true mean of y, for X=x0, is in the interval with (1−α)100% confidence.

Long description of Figure 15

A scatterplot of y against x with a solid fitted line rising from lower left to upper right. One value on the horizontal axis is marked with a dot and labelled x0. Directly above it a short vertical bar with horizontal end caps crosses the fitted line, centred on it. That bar is the confidence interval for the mean response at x0. Note that the bar is much shorter than the vertical scatter of the data points around the line: it is an interval for the average value of y at this predictor value, not for where a single new observation would land. The prediction interval defined next is the wider one that does the latter.

y^ (x0) ± t1−α/2,n−2 σ^ 1n+ (x0−x‾)2 SSX

Prediction interval for a new observation

An interval such that the next observation of y, ynew, for X=x0, will be in the interval with (1−α)100% confidence.

y^new (x0) ± t1−α/2,n−2 σ^ 1+ 1n+ (x0−x‾)2 SSX

Where the extra 1 comes from Because εi∼N(0,σ2), predicting a new observation carries two sources of variability, which add:

σ2 ⏟ new error + σ2 ( 1n+ (x−x‾)2 SSX ) ⏟ uncertainty in ŷ

The confidence interval for the mean response has only the second piece. That is exactly why the prediction interval is always wider.

III. Sum of squares decomposition

To check whether X is a significant predictor, we can use the residuals.

If we had no predictor at all, our best prediction of y would just be y‾. The decomposition compares that baseline against the fitted line.

Long description of Figure 16

The plot shows a horizontal dotted line at y‾ — the prediction you would make with no predictor available — and a solid rising fitted line crossing it. Several observations are scattered around. At one highlighted observation, three parallel vertical bars are drawn a few pixels apart so that all three are visible: a dotted bar running from y‾ up to the observation, a dashed bar running from y‾ up to the fitted line, and a solid bar running from the fitted line up to the observation. The dashed and solid bars stack end to end to make the dotted one, which is the whole content of the identity below: the distance from the mean to a data point is the distance the regression line explains plus the distance it fails to explain. Each bar is distinguished by dash pattern as well as colour, and the legend names all three. In the original notes these were drawn in purple, red and green respectively.

∑i (yi−y^i)2 + ∑i (y^i−y‾)2 = ∑i (yi−y‾)2
The three sums of squares
Name Formula What it measures
SSE (error) ∑i(yi−y^i)2 what the line does not explain
SSR (regression) ∑i(y^i−y‾)2 what the line does explain
SST (total) ∑i(yi−y‾)2 total variability in y

Two extreme cases

Long description of Figure 17

Panel 1 shows four points lying exactly on the rising fitted line. Every residual is zero, so ∑i(yi−y^i)2=0, which is SSE, and since yi=y^i the regression sum of squares equals the total. Here SST is made up of a lot more SSR than SSE, and we reject the null: the slope is real.

Panel 2 shows the opposite. The fitted line is nearly flat and almost indistinguishable from the dashed horizontal line at y‾, while the points sit far above and below both. SST is made up of relatively more SSE than SSR, and we fail to reject the null. Reading the two panels together is the intuition behind the test statistic: it is a ratio, and what matters is which of the two pieces dominates.

IV. The F-test

SSR/1 SSE/(n−2) ∼ F1,n−2

Degrees of freedom in general With p the number of β's in the model, the numerator has p−1 degrees of freedom and the denominator has n−p. For SLR, p=2, which gives 1 and n−2.

The F distribution

Let U∼χk2 and V∼χm2, with U and V independent. Then

F= U/k V/m ∼ Fk,m
Long description of Figure 18

The horizontal axis is F and the vertical axis its density f(F). The curve starts at the origin, rises steeply to a peak in the left half, and then decays slowly to the right. A vertical line near the right-hand end marks the critical value F*; the region beyond it is hatched and labelled α, and the region to its left is labelled 1−α. Because the statistic is a ratio of two non-negative sums of squares it can never be negative, and only large values are evidence against the null — so the whole rejection region sits in one tail, unlike the two-tailed t test.

Why the ratio has an F distribution

SSRσ2 ∼ χ12
SSEσ2 ∼ χn−22

So, taking the ratio and letting the σ2 cancel,

F= SSR/1 SSE/(n−2) ∼ F1,n−2
Reading the statistic
If SSE is … relative to SSR then the slope is …
largebigger share of SSTnot significant
smallsmaller share of SSTsignificant

Decision rule

F> F1−α,1,n−2*

then reject H0 and say "X is a significant predictor of y."

V. Coefficient of determination

R2 := 1− SSESST
Long description of Figure 19

Left panel. The four observations lie close to the rising fitted line, but well above and below the dotted horizontal line at y‾. So SSE is much smaller than SST, R2 is one minus something small, and R2≈1.

Right panel. The observations are scattered widely, sitting about as far from the fitted line as from the mean line. Now SSE is close to SST, so R2 is one minus something close to one, which puts it closer to 0. Between these two extremes lies the bound 0≤R2≤1.

0≤R2≤1

Interpretation. R2 is the proportion of variance in y explained by the model.

For SLR,

R2=ρ2
